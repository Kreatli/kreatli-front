# How to Vet a Clipper Before Hiring

To **vet a clipper** before hiring, check that their portfolio matches your niche and format, confirm the samples are really their work, run a paid test clip on your own footage, and watch how they communicate through one round of revisions. A polished highlight reel proves less than a single clip cut from your content under real deadlines.

This guide gives you a checklist for each stage, plus the red flags worth walking away from.

## Why vetting matters for clipping

Clipping is repetitive and brand-sensitive. A strong wedding filmmaker might miss podcast hooks, and a fast gaming clipper might ignore compliance rules for finance or health content. Poor vetting tends to show up as:

- Hooks that bury the payoff several seconds in
- Captions that drift out of sync after the first cut
- Framing and caption style that change from week to week
- Missed deadlines once volume grows from 5 to 25 clips

A small paid test costs far less than a month of clips you cannot use.

## Step 1: Write down your non-negotiables

Before you look at a single profile, list what a clipper must handle:

- **Niche:** podcast, gaming, coaching, ecommerce, IRL streams
- **Output:** 9:16, length range, caption style
- **Volume and turnaround:** for example, 15 clips per week within 48 hours
- **File handoff:** how you share source footage and receive finished clips
- **Revisions:** how many rounds are included

Put this in your job post so clippers can filter themselves. The [clipper job post template](/guides/how-to-write-a-clipper-job-post) has a ready-made structure.

## Step 2: Check the portfolio with a clipping lens

Do not grade on cinematic color alone. Watch each sample on a phone and check:

1. **Vertical samples:** at least three clips in 9:16, not only horizontal showreels
2. **Hook timing:** does something happen in the first one to two seconds? ([why the opening matters](/glossary/video-hook-first-3-seconds))
3. **Moment selection:** is it the best part of the source, or just the loudest?
4. **Captions:** readable on mobile, spelled correctly, and clear of faces and game UI
5. **Audio:** clean levels, with loud reactions that do not distort
6. **Niche match:** talking-head work if you run a podcast, gameplay if you stream
7. **Consistency:** several clips that feel like the same professional made them

## Step 3: Confirm the work is theirs

Portfolios can be borrowed or inflated. Ask simple questions:

- **"Which parts of this clip did you do?"** Some clippers edit, others only pick moments, and some work under a team lead.
- **"Can you show the source timestamp?"** A clipper who chose the moment can usually tell you where it came from and why.
- **"Which clip performed best, and why?"** Good clippers explain their choices in specifics.

Specific, confident answers are a good sign. Vague answers about "big creators I've worked with" are not.

## Step 4: Run a paid test clip

Give your top two or three candidates the **same short source segment** and the same brief. Ask for one to three clips and pay your normal rate: simple clips typically cost **$15–$35 each**, and complex gaming or motion edits **$50–$75+**. See [how much to pay clippers](/guides/how-much-to-pay-clippers) if you are unsure what is fair. Free tests attract the wrong applicants and waste good clippers' time.

Score each test on:

| Criterion | What good looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Moment choice | Picked a segment that works for a cold viewer |
| Hook | A reason to keep watching within the first two seconds |
| Brief adherence | Length, caption style, and format match what you asked |
| Deadline | Delivered on time without reminders |
| Revision response | Applied one round of feedback accurately and quickly |
| File hygiene | Clear file names, correct aspect ratio, no watermarks |

Using the same source for every candidate makes the comparison fair. Often the difference is judgment, not technical skill.

## Step 5: Check history and references

- **Reviews and completed jobs** on the clipper's profile, if available
- **Repeat clients:** creators who kept working with the same clipper are a strong signal
- **Scope honesty:** do they turn down work outside their niche, or say yes to everything?

If a past client is reachable, one short message asking about reliability and communication is worth sending.

## Red flags that should end the conversation

- **Unpaid "trial" batches** that the clipper offers at large volume, or that you would be asking for
- **Requests for your account password** when the job is to deliver files
- **Pressure to pay a large amount upfront** before any work on your footage
- **No questions at all** about your audience, references, or style
- **Inconsistent samples** that look like they came from several different editors
- **Promises of guaranteed views** or viral results, which nobody can deliver

For more on spotting scams on either side, see [is clipping legit](/guides/is-clipping-legit).

## Step 6: Agree terms before scaling

Before you move from a test to weekly work, confirm in writing:

- Pay type and rate: per clip, per batch, hourly, or monthly
- Turnaround and time zone overlap
- Who supplies or licenses music and stock assets
- Confidentiality for unreleased content
- How either side ends the arrangement, and with how much notice
- Payment method and timing (Kreatli does not process payments, so you and the clipper agree on this directly)

## FAQ

### How many sample clips should I ask a clipper for?

Three to five vertical samples in your niche are enough for screening. The real decision should come from a paid test on your own footage, because samples can be cherry-picked.

### What questions should I ask a clipper before hiring?

Ask how they find moments in a long episode or stream, what their usual turnaround is for a batch of ten clips, how they handle revisions, and whether they have clipped content like yours. Listen for specific answers, not general promises.

### Should I hire the cheapest clipper?

Not by default. The lowest rate often costs more in revisions and missed deadlines. Choose the best test result for the price, then confirm the clipper can repeat it every week.

### How long should a test clip take?

For one to three simple clips from a short segment, 24 to 72 hours is a reasonable window. Agree the deadline upfront, since meeting it is part of what you are testing.
