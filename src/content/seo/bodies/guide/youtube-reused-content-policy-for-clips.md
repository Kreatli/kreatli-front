# YouTube Reused Content Policy: What It Means for Clips and Clip Channels

YouTube's **reused content policy** is a monetization rule, not a copyright rule. If a channel mostly reposts other people's content without adding much of its own, YouTube can keep it out of the Partner Program or remove its ads. The rule looks at your **whole channel**, and it applies **even if you have permission**. For clips, the test is simple: viewers should see a clear difference between the original and your video.

This guide explains the policy for clippers and clip channel owners. It is based on YouTube's help pages as read on November 3, 2026. It is general information, not legal advice. YouTube's [channel monetization policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392) are the source of truth.

## What YouTube counts as reused content

YouTube says reused content means taking content that is already on YouTube or elsewhere online and posting it again without adding much. "Adding much" means real commentary, real edits, or something that teaches or entertains. YouTube also calls this duplicate or scraped content.

Reviewers look at the whole channel: the videos, the titles, the descriptions and the channel description. If they can't tell the content is yours, the policy can apply.

### What YouTube says is usually fine

YouTube's examples of allowed reused content include:

- Using clips for a **critical review**
- **Reaction videos** where you comment on the original
- Sports replays where you **explain the moves** a competitor made
- Edited footage from other creators where you **add a storyline and commentary**
- Content that **primarily features the uploading creator**
- Edited footage with audio and visual effects that shows **substantive editing** unique to your channel

### What YouTube says is not fine

YouTube's examples of what breaks the policy include:

- Clips of moments from a show **edited together with little or no narrative**
- Short videos **compiled from other social media** sites
- Content **uploaded many times** by other creators
- **Promotion of other people's content, even with permission**
- Content downloaded or copied **without substantive modifications**
- Videos that get views mostly from **non-verbal reactions** with no voice commentary

Both lists come from YouTube's help page and are not complete. YouTube says so itself.

## Why clip and compilation channels get flagged

A typical fan clip channel posts the best moments from someone else's streams with a caption and a title. That matches YouTube's "not allowed" list. The moments belong to someone else, the edits are light, and it's hard to tell what the channel owner made.

Three things make it worse:

1. **Volume.** Posting dozens of near-raw clips a week makes the whole channel look like reuploads.
2. **Duplicates.** Popular stream moments get clipped by many channels. YouTube lists content uploaded many times by other creators as a problem.
3. **Thin channel signals.** A channel name, description and titles that only name the streamer give reviewers nothing that shows your own contribution.

## Permission does not solve reused content

This is the part most clippers miss. YouTube says the policy applies **even if you have permission** from the original creator. It is **separate from copyright**, so permission and fair use don't change the answer.

So there are two separate questions:

A clip can pass the first test and fail the second. A streamer's written permission helps protect you from a takedown. It does not make a channel of lightly edited clips eligible for ads.

## Official clip channels vs. fan clip channels

[Clip channels](/glossary/clip-channel-meaning) come in two kinds, and the policy lands differently on each.

- **Official clip channels** belong to the creator, or are run for them. They post the creator's own content. YouTube lists videos that mainly feature the uploading creator as allowed. When a creator hires a clipper to post on the creator's own channel, the content is the creator's own work.
- **Fan clip channels** post someone else's content on a channel the clipper owns. Even with permission, YouTube treats promoting other people's content as reused content, unless the clips add real value of their own.

It is also a good reason for creators to [hire a clipper](/hire) who posts on the creator's own channel. The clips build the creator's audience, and the content is clearly theirs.

## What a "meaningful difference" looks like in a clip

YouTube doesn't publish a checklist, and nothing guarantees approval. Its examples point to clips that show the clipper's work, not just the streamer's moment:

- **Commentary or context.** A voiceover or on-screen explanation of why the moment matters.
- **A storyline.** Several moments cut into a narrative, such as a recap of how a challenge played out.
- **Substantive editing.** Real editing choices, effects and structure, not just a crop and a caption.
- **A clear channel identity.** A name, description and titles that show what your channel adds.

Captions alone are a weak signal. Every allowed example YouTube gives involves commentary, a story, or real editing. Word-for-word subtitles on an unchanged moment are a small change.

Turning one long video into many short ones is [repurposing content](/glossary/repurposing-content-meaning). That is fine on the creator's own channel. On someone else's channel, it is the pattern this policy targets.

## Does the policy apply to Shorts?

Yes. YouTube's Shorts monetization policies include the same reused content rules. YouTube also says some Shorts views don't count toward revenue sharing. Its examples are unedited clips from others' movies or shows, reuploads of other creators' videos, and compilations with nothing original added.

For clip channels this matters twice: the channel can be refused for the Partner Program, and non-original Shorts may not earn even on a monetized channel.

## What to do if monetization was denied for reused content

YouTube's guidance points to fixing the pattern across the channel, not one video.

1. **Read the email and YouTube's policy page.** Check which policy the decision names.
2. **Look at the channel as a reviewer would.** Find the videos with little of your own work in them.
3. **Change the pattern.** Remove or make private the weakest reuploads, and make new uploads clearly your own.
4. **Appeal or reapply.** YouTube's Partner Program page says you can appeal within 21 days. Or keep posting original content and reapply after 30 days. After a second rejection, the wait is 90 days.

Check YouTube's [Partner Program overview](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/72851) for the current rules before you act, because they change.

## How reused content affects clipping pay

The policy is about **YouTube ad revenue on your own channel**. It doesn't decide whether a creator or a campaign pays you. It does change which kind of clipping work is safest.

- **Fixed pay for a creator's own channel.** The creator hires you, you edit, and the clips go on their channel. You are paid for the edit, and the content is theirs. This model fits the policy best. Typical rates are in our [clipper pay rates guide](/guides/clipper-pay-rates).
- **CPM campaigns on your own accounts.** The campaign pays you per verified view, but the clips sit on your channel. YouTube can still refuse ads on that channel, so the campaign pay is your income, not ad money.
- **Fan clip channels for ad revenue.** This is the model the policy hits hardest. [Clip farming](/glossary/what-is-clip-farming) many near-raw clips is unlikely to be monetized on YouTube.

## FAQ

### Is clipping allowed on YouTube?

Posting clips is not banned, but two rules apply. Copyright decides if you can post someone's content at all. The reused content policy decides if a channel of clips can earn ad money. YouTube lists clips with real commentary, a story or real editing as allowed.

### Can clip channels be monetized on YouTube?

Some can. An official clip channel that posts a creator's own content is in a strong spot, because YouTube allows videos that mainly feature the uploader. Fan channels that repost other people's moments with light edits often fail the policy, even with permission.

### Does adding captions avoid the reused content policy?

Not on its own. YouTube's allowed examples all add commentary, a story or real editing. Captions that only repeat what was said are a small change. They are unlikely to make a channel of reposted clips eligible.

### Is reused content the same as copyright?

No. YouTube says the policy is separate from copyright. Permission and fair use don't decide it. A clip can have no copyright claims and still count as reused content, and a licensed clip can still fail.

### Is there a YouTube reused content checker?

We found no official checker from YouTube. Reviewers make the call when they review a channel for the Partner Program. The best check is to look at your channel as a reviewer would: would a viewer see what you added to each video?

### Does the reused content policy apply to YouTube Shorts?

Yes. YouTube's Shorts rules include the reused content policy. YouTube also says views on unedited clips of others' content, or compilations with nothing original, don't count toward Shorts revenue sharing.
