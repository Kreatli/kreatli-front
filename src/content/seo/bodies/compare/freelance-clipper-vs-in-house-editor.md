# Freelance Clipper vs. In-House Editor: Which Should You Hire for Short-Form Clips?

Choosing between a **freelance clipper vs. in-house editor** comes down to volume, budget, and how predictable your editing needs are. A freelance clipper turns your long content into Shorts, TikToks, and Reels at an agreed rate with no payroll commitment. An in-house editor is a salaried team member who handles editing as a full-time job.

For most growing creators, a freelance clipper is the right first hire. An in-house editor starts to make sense once short-form is one part of a large, steady, always-on production schedule.

## What a freelance clipper does

A [clipper](/glossary/what-is-a-clipper) is a short-form specialist. They review your podcasts, streams, or long videos, pick the strongest moments, and deliver finished vertical clips with hooks and captions. You post them on your own channels.

Freelance clippers usually work with several creators, so they see what hooks and formats are working across niches. You can hire one for a single batch, a weekly volume, or an ongoing monthly arrangement. Format-specific pages like [hiring a podcast clip editor](/hire/hire-a-podcast-clipper) and [hiring a YouTube Shorts editor](/hire/hire-a-youtube-clipper) cover what each specialty looks like.

## What an in-house editor does

An in-house editor joins your team as an employee. They learn your brand deeply, sit in planning meetings, use your tools, and handle editing as a core function. On a small team, that often means long-form edits, Shorts, thumbnails, and fast turnarounds on live content, all from one person.

The model works when editing is **always on**: daily uploads, same-day stream highlights, or a media business with continuous production.

## Cost: how to compare fairly

Comparing a salary to a per-clip rate is easy to get wrong. Compare the **full yearly cost** of each option for the same output.

**In-house.** Start with salary. The US Bureau of Labor Statistics reports a median annual wage of $75,420 for film and video editors as of May 2025. Social-first editors at smaller companies may earn less, and senior editors more. Then add employer payroll taxes, any benefits, a capable computer, software subscriptions, and the time you spend recruiting and managing.

**Freelance.** Multiply your weekly clip volume by the rate, then by 52. Freelance clippers commonly charge around $15 to $35 per clip for simple talking-head or podcast edits, and more for complex gaming or motion work. For example, 20 simple clips a week at $25 each is $500 a week, or about $26,000 a year. Your numbers will differ, so see [how much to pay clippers](/guides/how-much-to-pay-clippers) for current ranges.

The comparison shifts once you need more than clips. If you also need long-form edits, thumbnails, and same-day turnarounds, an in-house editor covers work a clipper would not.

## When an in-house editor makes sense

A full-time editor is usually the better choice when:

- You publish **daily or near-daily** across several formats
- Editing volume is **stable year-round**, not seasonal
- You want someone **embedded in strategy**, not just delivering exports
- You need **fast turnarounds during your working hours**
- You have the budget for salary and benefits, plus time to manage an employee

Established podcast networks, daily news channels, and larger creator teams often reach this point.

## When a freelance clipper makes sense

A freelance clipper is usually the better choice when:

- Short-form is your **main editing need**
- Your volume **changes month to month**, around launches, seasons, or tours
- You want a **specialist** for a niche, like gaming highlights or podcast clips
- You want to **test short-form** before committing to a hire
- You would rather pay for output than for fixed hours

Channels posting somewhere around 10 to 30 Shorts a week often sit in this range: too much to handle yourself, not enough to justify a full salary.

## Hidden costs on each side

**In-house:**

- Paying full salary in slow months
- A bad hire can take months to fix
- One generalist may not be strongest at short-form hooks
- Doubling output may mean a second hire

**Freelance:**

- Time spent writing briefs and giving feedback, especially early on
- Availability limits, since your clipper has other clients
- Finding a replacement if they move on
- Agreeing payment terms yourself; on Kreatli, payment is arranged directly between creator and clipper, and Kreatli does not hold escrow

## The hybrid path

Many creators start with a freelance clipper on a monthly arrangement and keep the same person week after week. If volume grows and you want them full time, you already know their work, speed, and communication. Some creators also keep a freelance clipper alongside an in-house long-form editor, so each focuses on what they do best.

## FAQ

### Is it cheaper to hire a freelance clipper or an in-house editor?

For most creators whose main need is short-form clips, a freelance clipper costs less, because you pay for the clips you need rather than a full salary, taxes, and benefits. Once you need daily output across several formats, an in-house editor can become better value.

### How much does an in-house video editor cost?

The US Bureau of Labor Statistics reports a median annual wage of $75,420 for film and video editors as of May 2025. The total cost to employ one is higher once you add payroll taxes, benefits, equipment, and software.

### Can a freelance clipper work like a part-time team member?

Yes. Many creators keep the same clipper every week on a monthly or yearly arrangement. On Kreatli you can set the pay type to monthly or yearly and the availability to part-time or full-time in your job post.

### When should I switch from a freelance clipper to an in-house editor?

Consider switching when your editing volume is high and steady all year, you need someone available during your working hours, and the yearly cost of freelance work approaches what you would pay to employ an editor.
